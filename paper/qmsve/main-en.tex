\begin{abstract}
We analyze minimum-singular-value estimation by repeated spectral filtering on the normalized right Gram operator $G=(A/\alpha)^\dagger(A/\alpha)$. Rather than assume a gap to control eigenstate fidelity, we derive the exact worst-case bias of the filtered Gram mean over finite spectra with a given minimum and initial target mass. The extremizer has only two eigenvalues. This yields uniform gap-free bounds with asymptotically sharp constants: the cosine-Gram response $\cos(\beta G)$ has mean bias of order $t^{-1/2}$, whereas the distinct dilation response $\cos(\beta\sqrt G)$ has order $t^{-1}$ at $t$ scalar iterations. The corresponding worst-case noiseless singular-value errors are of orders $t^{-1/4}$ and $t^{-1/2}$. Fixed-degree polynomial baselines attain the same rates. We also identify an exponential restart penalty caused by repeated global Taylor normalization and propagate success-block error through postselection and Bernoulli readout. Classical spectral audits check the sharp envelopes and the scaling penalty. These results refine the analysis of an existing proposal; they do not introduce a new quantum filtering primitive or establish a circuit-level advantage. Priority of the combined sharp analysis remains subject to further literature review.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
Extreme singular values arise in numerical linear algebra and in spectral questions connected to ground-state energy estimation. An earlier manuscript, \emph{Quantum maximum/minimum singular-value estimation algorithms} (author and publication metadata not stated in the source file; hereafter QMSVE) proposes quantum power iteration for the largest singular value, an inverse-iteration baseline for the smallest, and a cosine-based triangular iteration that avoids explicitly applying a matrix inverse \cite{QMSVE}. This report makes that proposal precise on a right-space operator, records the conditions needed for convergence, and checks a small set of finite-dimensional examples.

The main correction is about the operator being transformed. For a general rectangular matrix, applying $\cos(A)$ is not a valid singular-value prescription. We instead set $B=A/\alpha$ and $G=B^\dagger B$ on the right singular-vector space. The QMSVE cosine rule is then $C=\cos(\beta G)$, whose eigenvalues are $\cos(\beta\sigma_i^2/\alpha^2)$. This function is positive and decreasing on the normalized spectrum when $0<\beta<\pi/2$. A Hermitian-dilation construction gives a different response, $\cos(\beta\sigma_i/\alpha)$; we keep it separate.

The main question is whether a useful filtered-mean error bound survives without a spectral-gap promise. We answer it for specified responses by an exact two-point worst-case reduction and sharp asymptotic constants (Section~\ref{sec:gapfree}). A second outcome is a normalization audit and a finite-precision transfer bound (Section~\ref{sec:normalization}). The original geometric leakage bound is retained as background. These contributions improve the proposal's analysis rather than establish a new quantum algorithm.

\section{Related work and positioning}
\paragraph{Quantum linear algebra and power iteration.}
QSVT provides polynomial transformations of block-encoded singular values
\cite{Gilyen}. Quantum power iteration, inverse iteration, and QSVT-based
power methods already exist \cite{SekiYunoki,Kyriienko,Nghiem}; neither
iteration nor polynomial filtering is proposed as a new primitive here.
Nghiem et al. explicitly analyze small success probabilities and modify the
transformation to improve them. Their oracle construction differs from ours,
so our scalar restart analysis is not a rebuttal of their complexity result.

\paragraph{Cosine and Gaussian filters.}
Ge, Tura, and Cirac already use powers of $\cos H$, response-ratio bounds,
energy shifts, and amplitude amplification for ground-state preparation
\cite{GeTuraCirac}, notably Eqs.~(7)--(22) of their arXiv version.
Lu, Ba\~nuls, and Cirac use cosine filtering for finite-energy observables,
including reconstruction without physical filtered-state preparation
\cite{LuBanulsCirac}. He, Zhang, and Wang analyze a Gaussian filter and
explicitly compare it to cosine filtering in Appendix C \cite{HeZhangWang}.
Thus the cosine-to-Gaussian local approximation is established background.
A recent preprint also uses repeated power-cosine filtering with a
single-ancilla time-evolution construction \cite{Jo}. Its shifted/resonant
response and implementation assumptions differ from the unshifted Gram
response studied here; its performance claims are not adopted without audit.

\paragraph{Optimized projectors and input models.}
Lin and Tong optimize a polynomial projector for a known, isolated target
\cite{LinTongFilter}, and study near-optimal preparation and energy estimation
under additional information \cite{LinTongGround}. Dong, Lin, and Tong use
controlled time evolution via QET-U \cite{DongLinTong}. Their gap, energy
information, coherent depth, and amplification assumptions must be retained
when making resource comparisons. Our maximization over spectra for a fixed
response is distinct from minimax optimization over polynomials. Preserving
unit response at the target is already explicit in Lin and Tong's
construction; our normalization audit applies that principle to the Taylor
proxy of the QMSVE draft.

\paragraph{Classical context and the contribution claimed here.}
Gap-independent accuracy guarantees also occur in classical power and Krylov
methods \cite{MuscoMusco}; gap-free approximation is not a new concept.
The two-point extremization below is a simple linear-fractional argument
\cite{CharnesCooper}. The analytical contribution developed in this report is
the exact fixed-response mean-bias envelope, its asymptotically sharp
cosine-Gram versus dilation constants, and a joint audit of normalization and
accepted-state perturbations. We did not identify this combined statement in
the selected primary references; this bounded literature comparison does not
establish priority over all classical or quantum filtering literature.
The result is a modest analysis of specified estimators, not a new quantum
algorithm or an advantage over optimized projectors.

The unpublished QMSVE source is treated as provenance of the proposal, with
unconfirmed author and venue metadata \cite{QMSVE}. The separate prior
projects \cite{QCG2023,QCG2024} are not QMSVE implementations.
\section{Model and algorithm}
Let $A\in\C^{m\times n}$, with arbitrary positive $m,n$, and choose a known $\alpha\geq\|A\|_2>0$. Define
\[
 B=A/\alpha,\qquad G=B^\dagger B,\qquad
 0\leq \lambda_0\leq\lambda_1\leq\cdots\leq\lambda_{n-1}\leq1,
\]
where $\lambda_i=\sigma_i(A)^2/\alpha^2$ are the eigenvalues of $G$ in increasing order. Define the right minimum by $\sigma_0(A)=\min_{\|v\|=1}\|Av\|$. This includes right-kernel zero modes. For wide matrices it differs from the smallest entry of the conventional thin SVD, which may be positive. Estimating the smallest nonzero singular value requires an additional promise or access to the kernel-orthogonal subspace. For wide matrices ($m<n$), the right Gram operator necessarily has a kernel, so the same distinction applies.

Let $\ket{\psi}=\sum_i c_i\ket{v_i}$ be a normalized input state in a right singular basis and set $p_i=|c_i|^2$. For $0<\beta<\pi/2$, the ideal cosine-Gram filter is
\[
 C=\cos(\beta G),\qquad q_i=\cos(\beta\lambda_i)\in(0,1].
\]
The unnormalized state after $t$ iterations is $C^t\ket{\psi}$, its ideal success probability is
\[
 Z_t=\|C^t\ket{\psi}\|^2=\sum_i p_i q_i^{2t},
\]
and the normalized accepted state's spectral weights are $p_iq_i^{2t}/Z_t$. This is the QMSVE rule applied to the right Gram operator, with normalization made explicit.

\paragraph{Polynomial implementation model.}
For an integer $m_0\geq0$, let
\[
 P_{m_0}(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{m_0}\frac{(-1)^j(\beta x)^{2j}}{(2j)!},
 \qquad
 \delta_{m_0}=\frac{\beta^{2m_0+2}}{(2m_0+2)!},\qquad
 \widetilde P_{m_0}(x)=\frac{P_{m_0}(x)}{1+\delta_{m_0}}.
\]
The polynomial $P_{m_0}$ is the Maclaurin polynomial of $\cos(\beta x)$ through degree $2m_0+1$ (the odd coefficient vanishes). Taylor's theorem and the bound on every derivative of cosine give $|P_{m_0}(x)-\cos(\beta x)|\leq\delta_{m_0}$ for $|x|\leq1$. Hence $|\widetilde P_{m_0}(x)|\leq1$ on $[-1,1]$ and $\|\widetilde P_{m_0}-\cos(\beta\,\cdot)\|_\infty\leq2\delta_{m_0}/(1+\delta_{m_0})$; $\widetilde P_{m_0}$ is even of degree $2m_0$. It meets the boundedness and parity conditions for a real-polynomial Hermitian-QSVT response on a block-encoding of $G$; the completion and real-part extraction convention must still be fixed \cite{Gilyen}. This establishes an admissible polynomial response, not a phase sequence, gate synthesis, or a compiled circuit. As a polynomial in the singular value $\sigma/\alpha$, the response $\widetilde P_{m_0}((\sigma/\alpha)^2)$ has degree $4m_0$.

If a block-encoding of $B$ is available, a product construction can encode $G=B^\dagger B$ using two calls to the $B$ encoding, up to ancilla and reflection costs. We write $d_G$ for the actual oracle-query cost of one implemented cosine-polynomial transform of $G$, and $c_\psi$ for state preparation. Neither is numerically estimated here. A coherent implementation of $t$ transforms has query depth proportional to $t d_G$; a hard depth cap therefore constrains $t$ as well as the polynomial degree. The normalization bound $\alpha$ and its acquisition cost are inputs, not free outputs of this method.

\section{Finite-spectrum convergence and readout}
Let $T=\{i:\lambda_i=\lambda_0\}$ be the minimum-eigenvalue eigenspace, with initial mass $a=\sum_{i\in T}p_i>0$. Let $q_*=\cos(\beta\lambda_0)$ and suppose
\[
 q_i\leq r q_*\quad(i\notin T),\qquad 0\leq r<1.
\]
For the cosine-Gram response, if an eigenvalue strictly larger than $\lambda_0$ exists, define $\lambda_+=\min\{\lambda_i:\lambda_i>\lambda_0\}$; then $r=\cos(\beta\lambda_+)/\cos(\beta\lambda_0)<1$. If no such eigenvalue exists, all spectral weight is already in the target subspace and $F_t=1$.

\begin{theorem}[Conditional spectral leakage]
For every integer $t\geq0$, let $F_t$ be the probability mass of $T$ in the normalized state $C^t\ket{\psi}/\sqrt{Z_t}$. Then
\[
 1-F_t\leq\frac{1-a}{a}r^{2t}.
\]
If $\mu_t=\langle C^t\psi|G|C^t\psi\rangle/Z_t$, then
\[
 0\leq\mu_t-\lambda_0
 \leq(1-\lambda_0)(1-F_t)
 \leq\frac{1-a}{a}r^{2t}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\noindent The proof is given in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}. The inherited scalar Lean verification record is summarized in Appendix~\ref{app:lean}; the new sharp-envelope and stability theorems are hand-proved here and are not Lean-verified. The identification of the finite weights with a matrix eigenbasis, the block-encoding implementation, and the readout interpretation are finite-dimensional mathematical derivations, not part of the scalar Lean theorem.

\paragraph{Finite-degree transfer condition.}
The leakage theorem also applies to a bounded polynomial response $\widetilde q_i=\widetilde P(\lambda_i)$, but boundedness alone does not ensure that it orders the target eigenvalue correctly. Let $q_{\mathrm{out}}=\max_{i\notin T}q_i$ and let $E$ be a uniform error bound $|\widetilde P(x)-\cos(\beta x)|\leq E$ on $[0,1]$. If $E<(q_*-q_{\mathrm{out}})/2$, then $|\widetilde q_*|\geq q_*-E>0$ and $|\widetilde q_i|/|\widetilde q_*|\leq(q_{\mathrm{out}}+E)/(q_*-E)<1$. The generic finite-weight bound therefore transfers with this ratio. This sufficient condition can be conservative and is not evaluated as a degree-optimal filter here.

\paragraph{Maximum-singular-value branch.}
For the power method, take response $q_i=\lambda_i$ and target the maximum eigenspace $T_{\max}=\{i:\lambda_i=\lambda_{\max}\}$. If its initial mass is $a_{\max}>0$, $\lambda_{\max}>0$, and $\lambda_-=\max\{\lambda_i:\lambda_i<\lambda_{\max}\}$ exists, the same finite-weight inequality applies with $q_*=\lambda_{\max}$ and $r=\lambda_-/\lambda_{\max}$. Its target leakage is at most $((1-a_{\max})/a_{\max})r^{2t}$, and its filtered mean obeys $0\leq\lambda_{\max}-\mu_t\leq(1-F_t)\leq((1-a_{\max})/a_{\max})r^{2t}$. The maximum singular value estimate is $\alpha\sqrt{\widehat\mu_t}$ with corresponding error at most $\alpha\sqrt{((1-a_{\max})/a_{\max})r^{2t}+\zeta}$. If $\lambda_{\max}<1$, the unnormalized success probability is at most $\lambda_{\max}^{2t}$; this concentration step has a success cost as well.

Suppose a block-encoding of $B$ is used to measure the success flag without postselecting on it. On an input $\ket{\psi_t}$, that flag is Bernoulli with mean $\langle\psi_t|B^\dagger B|\psi_t\rangle=\mu_t$. From $M$ accepted filtered-state preparations, Hoeffding's inequality gives
\[
 |\widehat\mu_t-\mu_t|\leq \zeta
 \quad\text{with probability at least }1-\delta
 \quad\text{if}\quad
 M\geq\frac{\log(2/\delta)}{2\zeta^2}.
\]
Clip $\widehat\mu_t$ to $[0,1]$ and estimate $\sigma_0(A)$ by $\widehat\sigma=\alpha\sqrt{\widehat\mu_t}$. Then
\[
 |\widehat\sigma-\sigma_0(A)|
 \leq \alpha\sqrt{\frac{1-a}{a}r^{2t}+\zeta}.
\]
This is an additive-error statement under an ideal Bernoulli readout model; it does not account for noise, state-preparation error, or an unknown $\alpha$.

\paragraph{Success and cost accounting.}
For the exact ideal cosine response, the target component alone gives $Z_t\geq a q_*^{2t}$, while $q_i\leq q_*$ gives $Z_t\leq q_*^{2t}$. If each failed filter attempt is discarded and restarted from $\ket{\psi}$, the expected number of attempts per accepted copy is $1/Z_t$. For an implemented polynomial response $\widetilde P(G)$, the success probability is instead $\widetilde Z_t=\|\widetilde P(G)^t\psi\|^2$; a finite-degree cost estimate must use $\widetilde Z_t$, not silently substitute ideal $Z_t$. A query proxy for $M$ accepted readout samples is
\[
 M\left(\frac{c_\psi+t d_G}{\widetilde Z_t}+d_B\right),
\]
where $d_B$ is the cost of one Bernoulli readout using the $B$ block-encoding. This proxy is symbolic; finite-degree experiments below report only idealized spectral response probabilities and do not measure $\widetilde Z_t$ from a compiled circuit. Amplitude amplification, coherent recycling, and compiler-level gate costs are outside this accounting. In particular, when $\lambda_0>0$, $q_*<1$ and the success probability can decay exponentially in $t$; a small conditional leakage does not by itself imply low total cost.

For the near-degenerate example both responses remain close to $0.5$ at $t=16$. These numbers compare exact spectral responses only: their polynomial degrees, encodings, and compiled costs are not matched.


\section{Discussion and limitations}
The sharp envelope clarifies a limitation that eigenstate leakage alone conceals: a gap-free mean guarantee can hold even when no uniform target-fidelity guarantee is possible. Its exact leading constants expose the slower boundary behavior of the unshifted cosine-Gram response. The normalization example also shows why two identical conditional estimators can have exponentially different restart costs. These are estimator-specific analytical findings, not complexity lower bounds for general singular-value estimation.

The numerical audits use exact classical spectral information and ideal scalar success responses. They do not implement unknown singular-vector preparation, synthesize QSVT phases, or include physical noise. Positive target energy can cause exponential restart cost even when the scalar bias is small. Bernoulli/Hoeffding readout is only one readout model, and its precision dependence need not be optimal. A fair resource comparison must include state preparation, the normalization bound, block-encoding, polynomial degree, success amplification or restart, and readout. The power branch remains background; the new sharp results concern the minimum branch.

The two-point proof and polynomial baselines are elementary and overlap with established tools. The selected literature comparison supports a narrow analytical positioning, not a universal priority claim. Further review against classical weighted power-iteration analyses and additional cosine-filter papers is necessary before presenting the combined result as publishable novelty. Author metadata and original-source provenance also remain to be completed.

\section{Conclusion}
We derived an exact two-point envelope for filtered-mean bias and obtained sharp gap-free rates for specified cosine and polynomial responses on the right Gram spectrum. The analysis separates state fidelity, scalar estimation error, and acceptance cost. It identifies a concrete exponential penalty from repeated global normalization and supplies a postselection-aware perturbation bound. These results support a focused analytical paper draft; circuit realization, matched resource comparisons, and independent novelty assessment remain open.

\section{Proof of the finite-spectrum bounds}\label{app:proof}
Write $Q_T=\sum_{i\in T}p_iq_*^{2t}$ for the unnormalized target weight and $Q_{\perp}=\sum_{i\notin T}p_iq_i^{2t}$. The assumed response bound implies
\[
 Q_{\perp}\leq r^{2t}q_*^{2t}\sum_{i\notin T}p_i
 = (1-a)r^{2t}q_*^{2t},\qquad
 Q_T=aq_*^{2t}.
\]
Since $F_t=Q_T/(Q_T+Q_{\perp})$, we have
\[
 1-F_t=\frac{Q_{\perp}}{Q_T+Q_{\perp}}
 \leq\frac{Q_{\perp}}{Q_T}
 \leq\frac{1-a}{a}r^{2t}.
\]
Every eigenvalue outside $T$ lies in $[\lambda_0,1]$, so the filtered mean satisfies
\[
 0\leq\mu_t-\lambda_0
 =\sum_{i\notin T}\frac{p_iq_i^{2t}}{Z_t}(\lambda_i-\lambda_0)
 \leq(1-F_t)(1-\lambda_0)
 \leq\frac{1-a}{a}r^{2t}.
\]
For a readout estimate with $|\widehat\mu_t-\mu_t|\leq\zeta$, clipping to $[0,1]$ cannot increase its distance from $\lambda_0\in[0,1]$. Thus $|\widehat\mu_t-\lambda_0|\leq\mu_t-\lambda_0+\zeta$. For nonnegative $x,y$, $|\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}|\leq\sqrt{|x-y|}$, giving the stated singular-value error bound after multiplication by $\alpha$.
